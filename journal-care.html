Vinyl care without the anxiety

Filed under guides. September 22, 2026, by Rosa Trevino.

Every week somebody handles a record at my counter like it is made of soap bubbles and ancient prophecy. And every week the same person asks whether the faint marks in the light are scratches. They are usually not scratches. They are almost never scratches. Let’s fix the fear first, then the records.

The three things that actually ruin records

Short list, because the real list is short.

  • Heat. A record on a car dashboard in July is a record that will never be flat again. Vinyl warps around 140°F and it does not ask permission first.
  • Sleeves. Paper inner sleeves shed and scuff. Every year of storage in a rough paper sleeve does more damage than a hundred careful plays.
  • Gravity and pressure. Records stacked flat bend. Records stored leaning warp. Upright and snug, like books, is the whole secret.

That is it. Dust is manageable, fingerprints wash off, and the marks that scare you in raking light are usually sleeve scuffs that do not reach the groove walls at all.

What you can stop worrying about

Touching the surface. Holding a record by edge and label is good practice, but a thumbprint on the playing surface washes off with a record brush and cleaner. It is not a wound. It is a smudge.

Inner-sleeve swirl. Those fine marks under a bright bulb look devastating and usually inaudible. The groove is deeper than the scuff. Play the record. If you cannot hear it over the music, it does not exist.

Playing a VG record. A visually worn record that plays fine is fine. Grades describe how it sounds to us, not a moral judgment about how you treat your things.

Playing it “too much.” A well-adjusted stylus on a clean record takes thousands of plays to show wear. The records that die young die from bad needles, not from love.

The five-minute routine worth doing

  1. Carbon-fiber brush before every play. Three spins, light pressure. This is the whole daily routine.
  2. Change paper inners for poly-lined sleeves as you touch each record. Do it gradually, crate by crate.
  3. Store everything upright. Full shelves, no leaning, away from radiators and sunny windows.
  4. Deep-clean a record only when it is visibly dirty or audibly noisy. Wet cleaning is a tool, not a religion.
  5. Replace your stylus on schedule. A worn stylus is the single most destructive object in your house and it costs $30 to retire.
The stylus thing bears repeating: a worn needle grinding through a groove is the only way a careful listener destroys their records. Check it yearly.

What we do at the counter

Every used record that goes on our wall gets played, front and back, before it gets graded. If we hear a tick on track three, the tag says so. The grade is the record’s report card, not its eulogy, and it is written by people who genuinely want you to play this thing every Sunday for thirty years.
